## Findings

### J1-001 · HIGH · Criteria 3 (accuracy), 6 (recommendations follow from evidence), 1 (target users)
**Issue.** O3 (Rank 3) says Awaab's Law "appl[ies] to all registered providers regardless of size — VERIFIED [37]" (§3 O3, line 144). It then builds the target universe and ranking on "~1,100 small private registered providers (<1,000 homes)" (§2 row O3 line 65; §3 line 146; A-08 line 370; §5 "Why third" line 335: "~1,100 small providers are plausibly underserved"). The artifact leaves out a scope exclusion that appears in its own source [37], which it lists as "P, Read".
**Evidence.** GOV.UK Awaab's Law Phase 2 guidance: the law applies to "almost all social housing occupied under a tenancy and let by a registered provider". It "does not apply to any housing that is occupied under a licence", and it also excludes shared ownership and long leaseholds. The Almshouse Association (28 Oct 2025, https://www.almshouses.org/news/update-awaabs-law/) states that almshouse charities which are registered providers are exempt because residents occupy under licence. It quotes the guidance as excluding "temporary accommodation, supported accommodation, or other housing that is occupied under a licence". Snippet-level data from the Almshouse Association puts ~300 almshouse charities among RSH-regulated registered providers, averaging ~28 units. These are the very small, volunteer-run providers that the artifact's ASSUMPTION ("many small providers are volunteer- or part-time-run", line 146) and HYPOTHESIS A-09 describe.
**Why it matters.** The Rank 3 recommendation rests on a small-provider universe that is materially overstated. Part of the segment the artifact pictures, the smallest volunteer-run providers, is legally out of scope. Market Validation (Agent 2) would sample and interview the wrong population, and the ranking of O3 against O4 is not supported as written.
**Exact correction required.** (1) In §3 O3 "Problem", restate applicability accurately: tenancies of social housing let by registered providers, with no size threshold, excluding licence-occupied housing (including almshouses and licence-based supported or temporary accommodation), shared ownership and long leaseholds. Cite [37] plus the Almshouse Association page. (2) In §2 row O3, §3 "Target users", A-08 and §5 "Why third", replace "~1,100 small PRPs" with an explicitly labelled upper bound (SUPPORTED INFERENCE). Name the excluded groups. Label the in-scope count UNKNOWN until checked against the RSH register or data tables (almshouse count at snippet level only). (3) Revise A-09 and the "volunteer- or part-time-run" ASSUMPTION so they do not rely on exempt almshouses. (4) Re-state in §5 whether O3 still ranks third and why, or demote it, with the reasoning written out.
**Responsible agent.** Agent 1

### J1-002 · MEDIUM · Criteria 2 (competitor discipline), 6
**Issue.** For O3 the artifact names only enterprise HMS or CX vendors, a law-firm tool, and HazardClock, which it describes as pre-launch and as "a competitor (HazardClock) [that] targets exactly this niche" (§5, line 335; §3 line 158). From this it infers that small providers are "plausibly underserved". The artifact does say that lists are non-exhaustive (§1.3), but the "underserved" inference still drives the ranking.
**Evidence.** Rentalize Core targets "roughly 50 to 5,000 homes", includes Awaab's Law timescales and is priced "from EUR 199 a month" (https://rentalize.com/best-housing-management-systems-uk-2026/, 6 Jul 2026). The same article names PyramidG2 (100+ social landlords) and Landlord Vision as options for very small social landlords. HousingSurvey Pro is a live, self-serve damp and mould evidence timeline for housing associations and councils (https://housingsurvey.pro/). Made Tech offers hazard case management (https://www.madetech.com/blog/awaabs-law-phase-2-what-it-covers-and-what-housing-providers-should-be-doing-now/). All of these are vendor self-descriptions.
**Why it matters.** Live products aimed at small providers, with published price points, weaken the "underserved" rationale for Rank 3. They also supply the price anchors that Agent 2 needs.
**Exact correction required.** Add these vendors to §2 row O3 and §3 O3 "Existing solutions", graded V with URLs. Change "plausibly underserved" in §5 to a HYPOTHESIS and cite the competitors found. Remove the implication that HazardClock is the only tool aimed at the niche. Add to the §5 key questions: "traction and pricing of Rentalize Core, PyramidG2, Landlord Vision, HousingSurvey Pro among <1,000-home providers".
**Responsible agent.** Agent 1

### J1-003 · MEDIUM · Criteria 2 (evidence discipline), 6
**Issue.** The O2 pain evidence is mischaracterised and then carried into the ranking. Line 126 cites Resolution Foundation "~900,000 workers not receiving their holiday entitlement" and the TUC figure "~£2bn of holiday pay lost per year". §5 (line 325) turns these into "documented, widespread underpayment". A-05 ("Many UK SME payroll setups calculate variable-pay holiday incorrectly") is labelled SUPPORTED INFERENCE partly on [31].
**Evidence.** RF (Apr 2023) counts workers "reporting they had no paid holiday". The TUC counts 1.1 million employees who "did not get a single one of the 28 days' paid holiday … last year" and attributes this to denied requests, a culture of fear and workloads (see S18). Neither figure measures errors in holiday-pay calculation for variable-hours workers, which is the problem O2 would solve. The TUC figure is undated in the artifact and describes one year, not an annual rate. That also conflicts with the header claim that "every number quoted is attributed to a source and dated" (line 22).
**Why it matters.** Rank 2 partly rests on evidence of a different problem. A-05 overstates the support for the O2 core value proposition.
**Exact correction required.** Re-describe [31] accurately and with dates: RF Apr 2023, ~900k reporting no paid holiday; TUC figure giving its report date, 1.1m with no paid holiday in the prior year, ~£2bn. State that these show leave denial, not calculation error. Relabel A-05 as HYPOTHESIS, or support it with direct evidence of calculation errors. Re-word the §5 Rank 2 rationale to match.
**Responsible agent.** Agent 1

### J1-006 · MEDIUM · Criterion 5 (MVP-feasibility filter), Rank 1
**Issue.** O1's MVP-feasibility verdict is "Yes" (line 116), but it does not address conditions specific to the primary segment the artifact names: connected hardware, IoT and firmware makers (line 97). (a) Firmware and embedded C/C++ products often have no package-manager SBOM. Producing one usually needs binary analysis, which is the core of ONEKEY's offering (line 106). The MVP as described only ingests SBOMs. (b) "Deterministic version-range matching" (line 114) across ecosystems, especially CPE-based matching for embedded components, is a known precision problem. (c) Feed licensing (A-03, UNKNOWN) is a precondition for feasibility but sits outside the verdict.
**Why it matters.** This hard filter decides whether the Rank 1 candidate is viable for a small team in weeks. The verdict may hold for package-managed software vendors but not for the hardware and firmware segment that is named first.
**Exact correction required.** Make the O1 feasibility verdict conditional on segment: feasible for vendors that can supply CycloneDX or SPDX SBOMs, and UNKNOWN or not MVP-feasible where SBOM generation from binaries is needed. State what the MVP ingests. Carry A-03 into the verdict as a gating UNKNOWN. Add to the §5 Rank 1 key questions: "Do target customers already produce SBOMs?"
**Responsible agent.** Agent 1
